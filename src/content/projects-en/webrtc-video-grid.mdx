## Background

Because we received every participant's tracks simultaneously, 50-person rooms were practically impossible on mobile and mid-range devices. The CPU and GPU couldn't handle 50 decodes at once, and mobile devices shut down from overheating.

What made the problem harder was that *the quality-decision logic was scattered inside the adapter*. With no single place defining which resolution to use under which conditions, every round of tuning added more if statements, and regressions were frequent.

## Approach

<RevealOnScroll client:visible>
  I started by **cutting reception down to page units**. I unified page state in a single place in the store, and added the modern `setReceiverConstraints` API to the adapter so that only participants on the current page are received at high resolution.
</RevealOnScroll>

I split the quality policy out into a separate pure-function module. Three functions (computing receive resolution · send resolution · degraded resolution) take dpr · participant count · connection-quality as inputs and return a resolution bucket. The policy is decided in one place, the module, and the adapter only applies the result.

> One testable function instead of scattered if statements. Tuning becomes a matter of changing the function's inputs, and regressions get caught in tests.

Screen sharing runs outside the policy as a separate track (1080p / 10–15fps + `contentHint`). Background tabs pause sending and receiving, and network recovery is handled with `navigator.onLine` plus exponential-backoff reconnection.

I believed verification had to be observable from the outside. I wrote Puppeteer fake-camera bots and WebSocket bots myself, ran burst · steady · churn load in 6 · 17 · 25+ participant scenarios, and saved the simulcast layers, whether `setReceiverConstraints` was applied, and p95 metrics as JSON.

## Results

- Restored mobile usability in 50-person rooms. SFU forwarding blocked for participants outside the current page.
- VP9 preferred + VP8 fallback. Dedicated 1080p · 10–15fps for screen sharing.
- 20+ new regression tests covering page state, participant activation, the quality adapter, and VP9 / p2p off / bridge channel guards.
- Load-bot artifacts make simulcast application and quality-policy results externally observable on every build.

## What I Learned

The more branching an area has, like WebRTC, the more the answer was to *treat policy as data and extract it into a function*. The adapter becomes a thin layer that only applies the *result* of the policy, and tests only need to hit the policy function.

I was also reminded that verification has to be possible *from the outside*. Rather than trusting the SDK's internal state, I generated real send/receive traffic with fake-camera bots and tied the resulting metrics to build artifacts — and quality-policy changes started showing up on a graph.
