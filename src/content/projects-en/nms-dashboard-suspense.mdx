## Background

The NMS-Web frontend was a codebase I took over solo during its initial build-out phase (the backend stayed with a separate teammate). At handover, the dashboard was the first screen operators and clients saw after logging in, and it fired 5–7 widgets simultaneously — incidents, ports, systems, map, Top-Nodes and more. The entire page waited on one loading spinner, so fast widgets were held hostage to the slowest one.

The map data grew heavier in proportion to the number of sites, and every time a card click opened a popup, it re-fetched nearly the same information as the list. My first major task after the handover was to rework this data-loading architecture.

## Approach

<RevealOnScroll client:visible>
  I started by **cutting the loading boundaries into smaller pieces**. I standardized `useQuery` on `useSuspenseQuery` and placed a `<Suspense fallback>` boundary per widget, breaking the page-level spinner down into section-level ones. Fast widgets no longer waited on slow ones.
</RevealOnScroll>

I changed the map popup so that a card click no longer hits a separate API, and instead reuses the cached result of the list call as-is. I switched marker icons from external SVG fetches to base64 inline, eliminating extra resource requests.

I abstracted the infinite-scroll logic repeated on every page into a generic provider factory. Context, pagination and `getNextPageParam` behavior are sealed in one place, and pages inject only queryKey and queryFn.

> Instead of repeating the same pattern across many pages, building it well once and re-injecting it turned out to be the fastest path.

## Results

- Single page spinner → per-widget partial loading. Fast widgets no longer wait on slow ones.
- **0** extra network calls when opening the map popup (cache reuse).
- External SVG requests for marker loading eliminated.
- Infinite-scroll use sites (incidents · messages · QnA · map sites, etc.) consolidated into a single provider.
- Split a 246-line single component into context · hooks · view, slimming it by about **80%**.

## What I Learned

The biggest realization from reworking the data-loading architecture was that *where you place a Suspense boundary is itself a UX promise*. With a boundary per widget, users can tell "which widget is slow"; with one around the whole page, they can't tell "what is slow." Same library, different promise.

One more thing: for patterns every page ends up repeating, like infinite queries, abstracting them well once lets later pages come in without paying the pattern cost. With a single use site the abstraction cost feels wasteful, but the moment you see 4–5, abstraction becomes the fastest path.
